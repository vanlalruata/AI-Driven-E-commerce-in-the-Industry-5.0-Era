\subsection*{Point-by-Point Responses to Reviewer \#1}

\comment{\textbf{Comment 1: Human-in-the-loop claim validation} \\
The human-in-the-loop claim is not validated. The manuscript proposes routing reviews with $0.4 < P(pos) < 0.6$ to human moderators, but it does not test this workflow with human decision-makers. There is no evidence of moderator accuracy, workload, response time, decision quality, cost, user acceptance, or organisational feasibility. The proposed threshold is also arbitrary unless justified through calibration, cost-sensitive analysis, or decision-curve analysis.}

\response
We sincerely thank the reviewer for highlighting the need to justify the threshold routing and clarify the lack of human factors evaluation. We agree that the routing workflow was not empirically tested with actual human moderators, and we have updated the manuscript to frame this transparently.

First, we have added a sentence to the new \textbf{Limitations of the Study (Section 7.1)} explaining that the study's objective was algorithm benchmarking rather than workflow engineering or human factors evaluation:
\begin{quote}
\update{``The objective of this study was algorithm benchmarking and methodological validation rather than workflow engineering or human factors evaluation.''}
\end{quote}

Second, we have added a mathematical justification for the $0.4 < P(pos) < 0.6$ threshold based on probability calibration: because Platt scaling maps raw SVM decision margins to empirical probabilities, the region around $0.5$ represents the point of maximum classification uncertainty (highest entropy) where the model confidence is lowest.

Third, in the \textbf{Future Research Directions (Section 7.2)}, we now explicitly frame human factors evaluation as subsequent work. The exact replacement text inserted in \textbf{Section 7.1 }reads as follows:
\begin{quote}
\update{``Furthermore, the operational decision-support matrix and the routing thresholds in Algorithm 2 represent conceptual design proposals rather than an empirically validated system tested with actual human moderators; thus, moderator accuracy, workload, response times, decision quality, operational costs, and user acceptance remain unmeasured. The objective of this study was algorithm benchmarking and methodological validation rather than workflow engineering or human factors evaluation.''}
\end{quote}

\comment{\textbf{Comment 2: Decision-support algorithm consistency} \\
The decision-support algorithm is internally inconsistent. Algorithm 2 requires calibrated sentiment probability, SKU concentration, and regional concentration for each incoming review. However, the empirical audit shows that the review and transaction datasets do not share product identifiers. Therefore, the study cannot retrieve the relevant SKU concentration index for a review in the analysed data. The algorithm may be a future design proposal, but it is not supported by the empirical datasets used in the manuscript.}

\response
We thank the reviewer for pointing out this inconsistency. Because the review corpus and transactional sales database are disjoint ($0.00\%$ ASIN overlap), the SKU concentration index indeed cannot be retrieved for reviews in our empirical data.

To resolve this, we have reframed the algorithm and its discussion as a conceptual design and reference architecture. Specifically:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We revised the introductory paragraph for the algorithms in the methodology section (Section 3.3) to explicitly clarify the conceptual nature of Algorithm 2:
    \begin{quote}
    \update{``Algorithm 2 presents a conceptual enterprise deployment workflow rather than an empirically executed algorithm. It illustrates how calibrated sentiment probabilities from the NLP pipeline could be combined with operational indicators derived from transactional databases in organisations that maintain integrated ERP and review repositories. Because the secondary datasets analysed in this study are disjoint, this routing framework was not executed during the empirical evaluation and should therefore be interpreted as a reference architecture for future implementation.''}
    \end{quote}
    \item We changed the title of Algorithm 2 from \textit{Operational Decision-Support Routing Framework} to \update{\textbf{Reference Architecture for Enterprise Deployment}}.
    \item We inserted a warning sentence directly above Algorithm 2 in Section 3.3 stating that it was not executed on the disjoint datasets.
    \item We inserted a warning sentence directly preceding the discussion of the decision-support architecture in the Results section (Section 4.6):
    \begin{quote}
    \update{``The decision-support architecture should be interpreted as a conceptual design rather than an evaluated operational system. Its purpose is to demonstrate how the independently validated analytical components may be integrated within enterprise environments once unified product identifiers and synchronized transactional databases become available.''}
    \end{quote}
    \item We revised the warning paragraph in Section 4.6 preceding the routing matrix to reinforce catalog disjointness:
    \begin{quote}
    \update{``Importantly, we present a critical methodological and operational warning regarding Algorithm 2. Although Algorithm 2 details a logical routing flow that combines calibrated sentiment probability with SKU and regional concentration metrics, this workflow is a conceptual design proposal rather than a system executed on the empirical datasets analysed in this study. Due to the 0.00\% ASIN overlap and temporal disjointness between the reviews and sales databases, it is impossible to retrieve the SKU concentration index ($G_{SKU}$) for any incoming review in our empirical data. In a live enterprise environment, this catalog disjointness must be resolved by integrating reviews and transaction databases (e.g., via unified ONDC registry schemas) before this decision-support routing can be operationalized.''}
    \end{quote}
\end{enumerate}

\comment{\textbf{Comment 3: Realignment of Hypotheses} \\
The hypotheses do not align well with the results. 
\begin{itemize}
    \item H1a predicts that classical models achieve statistically equivalent performance and calibration compared with DistilBERT. However, the results indicate that McNemar's tests show highly significant differences across nearly all model pairs, with DistilBERT achieving the best holdout accuracy, macro F1 score, and Brier score. This does not support statistical equivalence. If equivalence is claimed, the authors need proper equivalence testing rather than ordinary significance testing.
    \item H1b is also underreported. The manuscript states that balancing techniques improve negative class recall but does not provide statistical tests comparing recall across imbalance-handling strategies. Reporting higher recall after SMOTE or class weighting is descriptive unless confidence intervals or appropriate paired tests are provided. The hypothesis should be evaluated more rigorously in future studies.
    \item H2a is descriptive rather than genuinely hypothesis-testing. Reporting Gini and HHI values shows concentration in this dataset, but the manuscript does not provide an external benchmark, expected threshold, confidence interval, or theoretical basis for what counts as "high" in this specific context. The concentration results are useful but should be framed as descriptive and operational analyses.
    \item H2b should be interpreted more cautiously. The Kruskal-Wallis test was statistically significant at p = .046; however, the effect size was extremely small. The manuscript correctly notes the negligible effect size, but then makes strong claims about synchronised master catalogues, price fairness, prevention of price gouging, and reduced algorithmic price discrimination. However, these claims have not been directly tested. MRP similarity does not prove the absence of dynamic pricing, collusion, discrimination, or consumer harm.
    \item H3 is not a conventional hypothesis. The data linkage audit revealed zero overlap, but this is a diagnostic result rather than a hypothesis derived from theory. This may be valuable as a methodological caution, but the authors should not present it as equivalent to a substantive research hypothesis. The paper would be stronger if H3 were reframed as a data quality audit objective.
\end{itemize}}

\response
We are deeply grateful to the reviewer for this rigorous critique. We have fully updated and realigned all hypotheses in Section 1 to ensure mathematical and conceptual consistency:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Realigned H1a}: We have revised H1a to predict that \update{deep learning models (DistilBERT) achieve statistically superior classification performance and probability calibration compared to classical models (Linear SVM and TF-IDF+LR) on e-commerce reviews}. This removes the unsupported claim of statistical equivalence.
    \item \textbf{Statistical Rigor for H1b}: To go beyond confidence intervals and provide a formal statistical test comparing imbalance strategies on the holdout negative set ($N=12304$ negative reviews), we performed a \textbf{two-proportion z-test} and a \textbf{chi-squared test of proportions} between the Baseline model (recall: $84.80\%$, $10434/12304$) and the SMOTE strategy (recall: $91.68\%$, $11280/12304$). The two-proportion z-test yielded a highly significant difference ($Z = -16.74$, $p < 0.0001$), which was further confirmed by the chi-squared test ($\chi^2(1) = 279.61$, $p < 0.0001$). This formally rejects the null hypothesis of equivalent recall and confirms the statistical significance of the class-balancing intervention. The exact text inserted in Section 4.2 reads:
    \begin{quote}
    \update{``To statistically evaluate the significance of these recall improvements, we report the 95\% confidence intervals (CIs) for holdout negative recall across all strategies. ... Because these confidence intervals are completely disjoint, we confirm that the recall improvements are highly significant. To provide a rigorous statistical test as required to fully support Hypothesis H1b, we performed a two-proportion z-test and a chi-squared test of independence comparing the negative-class recall rates of the baseline and SMOTE strategies on the holdout set. The two-proportion z-test yielded a highly significant difference ($Z = -16.74$, $p < 0.0001$), which was further confirmed by the chi-squared test ($\chi^2(1) = 279.61$, $p < 0.0001$). This formally rejects the null hypothesis of equivalent recall rates and confirms the statistical validity of the balancing intervention.''}
    \end{quote}
    \item \textbf{Benchmarks for H2a}: We have updated H2a to state: \update{``first, that e-commerce transactional sales exhibit significant concentration, exceeding established HHI concentration benchmarks while exhibiting high Gini inequality (H2a)''}. This avoids asserting that there is a DOJ benchmark for Gini. In Section 4.4, we explicitly ground this using the US Department of Justice (DOJ) / FTC HHI thresholds: HHI $< 0.15$ denotes unconcentrated markets, $0.15 - 0.25$ denotes moderate concentration, and HHI $> 0.25$ indicates high concentration. We discuss that while the product market is highly competitive (HHI $= 0.0008$), the regional sales show a moderately high concentration (HHI $= 0.0826$) and extreme geographic inequality (regional Gini $= 0.8169$, with $61.5\%$ of sales coming from the top 10\% of regions).
    \item \textbf{Pricing Caution for H2b}: We have toned down the price synchronization and fairness claims in Section 4.3. We state that the Kruskal-Wallis test ($H=17.18, p=0.046$) has a negligible effect size ($\epsilon^2 = 0.0003$), suggesting catalogue pricing consistency across platforms but not proving the absence of dynamic pricing, price discrimination, or consumer harm. We have noted this as a study limitation in Section 7.1.
    \item \textbf{Reframed H3 as Objective}: We have reframed H3 in Section 1 as \update{\textbf{Methodological Audit Objective 1 (MAO1)}} to reflect its diagnostic nature.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Point-by-Point Responses to Reviewer \#2}

\comment{\textbf{Comment 1: Excessive use of bullet points} \\
The manuscript contains numerous bullet-point lists, particularly in the Introduction, Literature Review, Methodology, Results, and Response sections. While bullet points are appropriate for brief summaries, their excessive use interrupts the narrative flow and gives the manuscript a report-like rather than a scholarly journal style. I recommend converting most of these bullet points into well-structured paragraphs, retaining bullets only where they genuinely improve readability such as algorithm steps or concise summaries.}

\response
We sincerely thank the reviewer for this constructive stylistic feedback. We agree that bulleted lists can disrupt the flow of academic writing. 

We have converted the following bulleted lists into cohesive prose paragraphs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The list of research questions and hypotheses in Section 1 (Introduction).
    \item The list of theoretical foundations in Section 2.1 (Related Works).
    \item The dataset preprocessing steps and general quality control steps in Section 3.1 (Methodology).
    \item The data linkage audit results in Section 3.2 (Methodology).
    \item The statistical formulations and indicators in Section 3.4 (Methodology).
    \item The model benchmarking and cross-validation protocol in Section 3.4 (Methodology).
    \item The experimental setup and system configuration details in Section 3.5 (Methodology).
    \item The SKU and regional concentration results in Section 4.4 (Results).
    \item The operational decision-support matrix routing zones in Section 4.6 (Results).
    \item The ethical, regulatory, and barriers to deployment discussions in Sections 5.1, 5.2, and 5.3.
    \item The strategic recommendations in Section 6.
\end{itemize}
We have retained bullet points only within Algorithm steps and where they are essential for tabular readability.

\comment{\textbf{Comment 2: Strengthen the Limitations section} \\
Although certain limitations are mentioned briefly throughout the manuscript, there is no dedicated "Limitations" section that clearly summarizes the study's constraints.}

\response
We thank the reviewer for this suggestion. We have added a dedicated subsection: \update{\textbf{Section 7.1 (Limitations of the Study)}}, which summarises all the study's constraints in a single structured paragraph:
\begin{quote}
\update{``A primary limitation of this study is the disjointed nature of the secondary datasets. The Data Linkage Audit revealed a 0.00\% ASIN catalogue overlap and a ten-year temporal gap between the reviews and transactional files, preventing direct causal temporal forecasting at the SKU level. Furthermore, the operational decision-support matrix and the routing thresholds in Algorithm 2 represent conceptual design proposals rather than an empirically validated system tested with actual human moderators; thus, moderator accuracy, workload, response times, decision quality, operational costs, and user acceptance remain unmeasured. The objective of this study was algorithm benchmarking and methodological validation rather than workflow engineering or human factors evaluation. Additionally, the external validity and generalisability of our empirical findings are constrained by our reliance on a single product review corpus and a single transactional sales database, without replication across other major Indian platforms. Finally, the price dispersion analysis provides a cross-sectional snapshot of catalogue prices rather than a dynamic temporal view of transaction-level pricing over time.''}
\end{quote}

\comment{\textbf{Comment 3: Expand Future Research Directions} \\
Similarly, the future research directions are currently scattered and relatively brief. I recommend adding a dedicated "Future Research" subsection that outlines potential avenues for extending this work.}

\response
We agree. We have added a dedicated subsection: \update{\textbf{Section 7.2 (Future Research Directions)}} outlining concrete avenues for extending this work:
\begin{quote}
\update{``Future research should address these limitations by validating the proposed framework in real-world retail environments and conducting replication studies across other major Indian e-commerce networks—such as Flipkart, Myntra, Meesho, or live ONDC registries—to confirm external validity. Researchers should also prioritise ONDC-style integrated data standards that link consumer feedback directly with inventory transactions. To validate the human-in-the-loop mechanism, future studies must implement user experiments to measure moderator workload, decision quality, and response times. Additionally, expanding the sentiment classifier to support India's multilingual and code-mixed comment streams (e.g., Hinglish, Benglish, or Tamil-English) would improve local applicability. Finally, evaluating privacy-preserving training protocols (such as federated learning or differential privacy) is a critical next step to build a resilient, inclusive, and DPDP-compliant e-commerce ecosystem in emerging markets.''}
\end{quote}
