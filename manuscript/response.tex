\comment{\textbf{Comment 2: Lack of Grounding in Established Theories} \\
The paper reads more like a technical analytics project report than a fully developed research article with a robust theoretical contribution. The paper lacks anchoring in established theories (such as TAM, RBV, TOE, etc.).}

\response
To address this crucial point, we have reframed the manuscript as a rigorous academic research article grounded in established theories. We added a new subsection \textbf{Theoretical Foundations} (Section 2.1) that anchors our dual-empirical framework in five established organizational and technology adoption theories, citing relevant foundational studies: Barney (1991) for the Resource-Based View (RBV), Tornatzky et al. (1990) for the TOE framework, Davis (1989) for the TAM framework, Mahajan et al. (2023) for Stakeholder Theory, and Hirsch (1975) for Institutional Theory:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Resource-Based View (RBV) \& Dynamic Capabilities}: Framing the AI pipelines and real-time sentiment/sales models as firm-specific dynamic capabilities that enable platforms to sense consumer shifts and seize market pricing opportunities (citing Barney, 1991; Chen et al., 2022).
    \item \textbf{Technology-Organization-Environment (TOE) Framework}: Structuring the evaluation of model choices (accuracy vs. interpretability and compute constraints) and the compliance pressures of the environmental regulatory landscape (DPDP Act) (citing Tornatzky et al., 1990; Zhu \& Abd Rozan, 2026).
    \item \textbf{Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)}: Anchoring consumer trust, chatbot authenticity, warmth, and risk perceptions in the adoption of AI interfaces (citing Davis, 1989; Phan et al., 2025a; Zhu \& Abd Rozan, 2026).
    \item \textbf{Stakeholder Theory}: Discussing AI governance not as a cost-minimization tool but as an auditing system balancing the interests of consumers, platform sellers, and regulatory fiduciaries (citing Mahajan et al., 2023).
    \item \textbf{Institutional Theory}: Framing DPDP compliance as a coercive institutional pressure driving formal data audits (citing Hirsch, 1975).
\end{enumerate}
The revised text in Section 2.1 is highlighted in blue in the manuscript.

\comment{\textbf{Comment 4: Data Integration, Catalog Linkage, and Empirical Validity} \\
The integration between the Amazon reviews dataset (secondary data) and the Indian e-commerce transactional sales data is unclear. How do these datasets connect? If they are disjoint, linking them directly raises severe validity and scientific integrity concerns.}

\response
We thank the reviewer for raising this critical point, which goes to the heart of empirical research validity. Rather than asserting a false integration, we conducted a rigorous \textbf{Data Linkage and Catalog Audit} and transparently report that the two datasets are indeed disjoint. The audit revealed:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A unique ASIN match rate of exactly \textbf{0.00\%} (72005 unique ASINs in reviews vs. 7190 unique ASINs in sales).
    \item A ten-year temporal gap (reviews cover October 1999 to October 2012, whereas sales cover May 2022).
\end{itemize}
We have updated the manuscript (Abstract, Sections 1, 3.2, 4.6, and 7) to state this limitation explicitly. We now analyze these datasets as \textbf{parallel empirical components}: the review corpus is used to benchmark NLP sentiment pipelines, while the transactional sales dataset provides a localized case study of sales concentration and cross-marketplace pricing. This avoids unsupported causal claims (e.g., sentiment lagging sales) and models them as separate decision-support modules in a unified Industry 5.0 architecture. \update{This disjoint relationship and catalog discrepancy are visually demonstrated in the newly added Venn diagram (Figure 13) in Section 4.6 of the revised manuscript.}

\comment{\textbf{Comment 5: Sentiment Model Benchmarking and Sensitivity under Class Imbalance} \\
The TF-IDF+LR model is a simple baseline. The paper should evaluate and benchmark other standard models (such as Naive Bayes, Random Forest, XGBoost, Support Vector Machines) and deep learning models (such as transformers). Furthermore, e-commerce review datasets are heavily imbalanced (mostly positive). A model that fails to detect negative reviews is useless as an early-warning system. Sensitivity tests and class-balancing experiments must be provided.}

\response
To address this, we expanded our NLP modeling and benchmarking. We preprocessed 525,814 reviews and trained seven sentiment models. The revised Section 4.1 contains \textbf{Table 4}, which reports holdout accuracy, macro F1, negative-class recall, 10-fold cross-validation scores, Brier score (for probability calibration), and McNemar's pairwise significance tests. \update{Furthermore, to establish the comparative context and novelty, we added a State-of-the-Art comparison table (\textbf{Table 5}) benchmarking our results against eight prominent studies (Zhang et al., 2015; Fang \& Zhan, 2015; Ali et al., 2024; Sorour et al., 2024; Duru \& Sunar, 2025; Mamyrbayev et al., 2026; Daraghmi \& Zyadeh, 2026; Khan et al., 2026), comparing dataset scopes, accuracies, and algorithmic time complexities.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The deep DistilBERT model achieved the highest macro F1 (95.60\%) and negative recall (92.18\%) but requires high compute energy.
    \item The classical SVM (LinearSVC) achieved a high holdout accuracy of 96.53\% and F1-macro of 93.29\% with a Brier score of 0.0270, proving to be a highly sustainable, Green AI alternative.
    \item To address class imbalance, we conducted sensitivity experiments in Section 4.2 using five balancing techniques: Baseline, SMOTE, Class Weighting, Random Oversampling, and Random Undersampling. We present these in \textbf{Table 6}. Implementing SMOTE on TF-IDF+LR boosted minority negative recall from \update{84.80\% to 91.68\%}, with only a minor accuracy reduction (from \update{96.19\% to 95.78\%}), providing robust early-warning capability.
    \item \update{\textbf{Experimental Setup}: We added Subsection 3.5 detailing our standardized hardware and software configuration (Intel Core i9-10900K CPU, 16 GB DDR4 RAM, 512 GB SSD, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 8 GB GPU, Windows 11 Pro, Python 3.14). We explicitly clarify that all sentiment models benchmarked in this study were trained and validated on this identical setup, ensuring empirical and resource-efficiency consistency.}
\end{itemize}
\update{To support these findings, we have incorporated several diagnostic figures in Sections 4.1 and 4.2 of the revised manuscript: Figure 2 (Confusion Matrix for TF-IDF+LR), Figure 3 (ROC and PR Curves overlay for all classifiers), Figure 4 (reliability diagram for probability calibration), Figure 7 (10-fold cross-validation performance boxplots), Figure 5 (McNemar's pairwise significance p-value heatmap), Figure 6 (diagnostic error analysis of our proposed model), and Figure 8 (trade-off curves for class-balancing strategies).}

\comment{\textbf{Comment 6: Lack of Rigorous Statistical Validation of Results} \\
The paper presents descriptive statistics (e.g., top SKUs, average prices) but lacks formal statistical validation. The authors should calculate concentration metrics (Gini coefficients, HHI) for sales, run formal significance tests (e.g., ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis) for pricing dispersion across channels, and evaluate channel profitability differences with t-tests or non-parametric tests.}

\response
We have replaced all descriptive assertions with rigorous statistical tests in Section 4:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sales Concentration}: We calculated Gini coefficients and Herfindahl-Hirschman Indices (HHI). SKU sales show high concentration with a Gini of \update{0.6843} (top 10\% SKUs account for 54.3\% of revenue) but a low HHI of \update{0.000829} due to tail size. Regional sales are highly concentrated with a Gini of \update{0.8169} and HHI of \update{0.082636} (top 10\% states generate 61.5\% of sales).
    \item \textbf{MRP Dispersion significance}: Shapiro-Wilk testing confirmed non-normality, leading us to use the Kruskal-Wallis H-test. The test showed a statistically significant difference in MRP across 10 marketplaces ($H = 17.1753, p = 0.0460$). However, the effect size is negligible ($\epsilon^2 = 0.0003$). Dunn-Bonferroni post-hoc pairwise comparisons showed that only "MRP Old" vs. "Amazon MRP" and "Amazon FBA MRP" were significant ($p_{corr} = 0.0300$), indicating that marketplaces share a highly consistent master catalog.
    \item \textbf{Channel Profitability}: We analyzed unit profitability for INCREFF (mean profit: \rupee~15.07 $\pm$ \rupee~30.22, $N=10$). Shiprocket's data was entirely missing ($N=0$) in the files, preventing a formal t-test. We report this missing data transparently as a primary dataset limitation in Section 4.4.
\end{itemize}
Mathematical formulations for all these metrics are detailed in Section 3.4. \update{To visually validate these statistics, we have added several figures in Sections 4.3, 4.4, and 4.5: Figure 9 (top states and SKUs by revenue), Figure 10 (Lorenz curves representing revenue inequality at SKU and State levels), Figure 11 (MRP distribution boxplots across the 10 Indian marketplaces), and Figure 12 (unit profitability distributions).}

The discussion on the Indian Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act of 2023 needs to be strengthened. How do these data audits, model parameters, and disjoint datasets relate to compliance, purpose limitation, and consent?}
